** Geopolitics / Economics 3
The Lure of Specialization
	*hunter-gatherers were highly self-sufficient
	*agriculture produced the idea of specialization
		While hunter-gatherer societies had simple divisions of labor (often based on age or sex), the 
		sedentary nature of agriculture allowed for the intense, specialized, and often hierarchical 
		division of labor that characterizes complex civilizations (not everyone needed to produce food)
	*technology perfected specialization 

Specialization (at the human level) ---
Specialization of human labor increases productivity by allowing workers to become highly proficient at specific tasks, which reduces production time, improves quality, and fosters innovation. It eliminates time lost in task-switching, enables the use of specialized machinery, and allows for better matching of individual skills to tasks.

Key reasons for increased productivity include:
    Increased Dexterity and Skill: Repetition allows workers to master specific tasks, leading to faster and higher-quality production.
    Time Savings: By eliminating the need to switch between different tools, locations, and tasks, production flows more continuously.
    Technological Innovation: Specialized tasks make it easier to develop specialized, labor-saving machinery that automates specific steps.
    Efficient Allocation of Talent: Organizations can assign workers to tasks that best fit their abilities, maximizing output.
    Economies of Scale: Specialized labor enables large-scale production, which lowers the cost per unit. 

This, famously highlighted by Adam Smith using the example of pin manufacturing, shows that dividing labor into smaller, focused tasks leads to exponentially higher output compared to one person attempting all tasks.


Specialization (at a nation-state level) ---
	David Ricardo’s theory of comparative advantage (1817) argues that international trade benefits all countries if they specialize in producing goods with the lowest opportunity cost, rather than just goods they produce most efficiently. Even if one nation is less efficient at producing everything, it still gains by focusing on its relatively strongest industry. 

Key aspects of Ricardo's theory include:

    Relative Opportunity Cost: A country has a comparative advantage if it can produce a good by sacrificing fewer alternative goods than another country.
    Specialization and Trade: Countries should export goods for which they have a comparative advantage and import goods for which they have a comparative disadvantage.
    Beyond Absolute Advantage: Ricardo demonstrated that trade is mutually beneficial even when one nation has an absolute advantage in every product, contrary to previous thinking.
    Example (Wine and Cloth): The Library of Economics and Liberty points out that even if Portugal is more efficient at producing both wine and cloth than England, both nations benefit if Portugal specializes in wine (its highest advantage) and England specializes in cloth.
    Purpose: The theory was developed to argue against protectionist policies (like the British Corn Laws), promoting free trade to increase global efficiency and consumption. 

The theory remains a cornerstone of international trade theory, emphasizing that specialization based on relative, rather than absolute, superiority maximizes global output.

	The axioms and corollaries of standard economics
	
Economics: The study of choice and resource allocation under conditions of scarcity.

Axiom 1: As a society, our resources—land, labor, and capital—are insufficient to produce all the  goods and services we might desire. In other words, society faces a scarcity of resources.

	Theorem 1: Only in rare circumstance can we (individually) produce all of the goods and services  we 
	need. We are economically linked to others in hundreds—even thousands—of different ways.
	
	Theorem 2: All (functional) economies exhibit high degrees of specialization and exchange because 
	specialization and exchange enable us to enjoy greater production, and higher living standards than 
	would otherwise be possible.


Axiom 2: All production carries an opportunity cost: To produce more of one thing, society must shift resources away from producing something else. “There is no such thing as a free lunch.”
		
	Theorem 1. In a market system, individuals are not simply free to do what they want. Rather, they are 
	constrained by the resources they control.
	
		Corollary 1: We do not all start in the same place in the economic race. Individuals may have 
		more or less resources under their control than others.
