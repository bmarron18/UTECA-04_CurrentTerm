** Humor

--- II Theatrical and Performance Genres	---
In performance and literature, humor is also categorized by form and content. 
    ** Satire: Uses wit and irony to critique or mock societal shortcomings, institutions, or human folly. Examples include works like A Modest Proposal by Jonathan Swift.
    ** Farce: A form of low comedy that uses highly improbable situations, exaggerated characters, physical humor (slapstick), and chaotic plots for amusement.
    ** Romantic Comedy: A lighthearted plot centered on the development of a romantic relationship, typically ending with a happy conclusion.
    ** Black Comedy (Dark Humor): Finds humor in morbid or taboo subjects such as death, crime, or other sensitive topics. The humor arises from the discomfort and absurdity of the situations.
    ** Comedy of Manners: A form of high comedy that uses sophisticated language, wit, and subtle characterizations to satirize the behaviors and conventions of a particular social class.


---	III Other Specific Styles	---
    *** Observational Humor: Focuses on everyday life and social norms, pointing out the humor in common situations and shared experiences. Jerry Seinfeld's act is a classic example.
    *** Absurdist/Surreal Humor: Focuses on illogical events and bizarre situations, often defying rational explanation. Monty Python is a prime example.
    *** Slapstick: Relies on physical actions, pratfalls, and exaggerated violence for laughs (e.g., The Three Stooges).
    *** Anti-Humor (Anti-Joke): Intentionally unfunny or a joke with an anti-climax, where the humor comes from the expectation of a joke that never materializes.
    *** Deadpan: A form of delivery where humor is presented without emotion or a change in facial expression, forcing the audience to find the humor in the statement itself.

Notes
	- The Chinese concept of "Chinese" is complex 
	- The modern cultural concept of "Chinese," for example, must be understood in the context of 	
	China's recent political history
		1) China needed a modern identity to cope with conditions created by China's
	confrontation with the Western world where concepts of state, citizen are foreign
		2) the identity needs to acknowledge the traditional view of being at the center of
	existence. Chinese have viewed themselves as being at the center, surrounded by
	culturally inferior barbarians at the periphery.
	- Chinese have had an anthropocentric view is based on a deep-rooted sense of belonging to
	a unified civilization that boasts several thousands of years of uninterrupted history
	- Two pieces to Chinese identity 
		1) they feel a connectedness with the fate of China as a nation.
		2) they feel a sense of fulfillment, of being the bearers of a cultural heritage handed down 
		from their ancestors
	- Chinese are the Han, who ultimately absorbed the "barbarians". Because the Han had absorbed people of all languages, customs, and racial and ethnic origins; the meanings of being Chinese in the sense of ethnicity, culture, citizenship, or residence were almost never addressed. Al are Han.
	
	- By the early twentieth century this concept of a "Chinese people" included four major non-Chinese races, descendants of what were formerly referred to as barbarians: the Man (Manchus), the
Meng (Mongolians), the Hui (ethnic groups of Islamic faith in northwestern China), and the Zang (Tibetans)

	-the existence of a superior Chinese culture is, at best, a myth. The Chinese people and
Chinese culture have been constantly amalgamating, restructuring, reinventing, and reinterpreting themselves
